# Research - "Catching" Anxiety

## Summary

Scientists call the catching of another person's emotion "emotional contagion." The most cited definition describes it as the tendency to automatically mimic and synchronize facial expressions with another person, leading to emotional convergence.[^1] For stress in particular, researchers use "empathic stress," "stress contagion," and "stress resonance."[^2][^3][^4] In one lab study, 26% of people who only watched someone go through a stress test showed a significant cortisol rise of their own. The rate was 40% when the stressed person was their partner and 10% when it was a stranger.[^2][^5] Viewers' heart activity also changes with the stress level of a speaker they watch on video.[^3] Sweat from emotionally stressed people activated the amygdala of people who smelled it,[^6] but a related claim, that fear odors help people tell negative faces from neutral ones, failed to replicate in three attempts.[^7] Reviews say bodily syncing between people is real but that the research is fragmented and its meaning is unclear.[^8][^9]

Anxiety runs in families. Children of parents with anxiety disorders have higher odds of anxiety disorders themselves.[^10][^11] Twin studies find a genetic share in anxiety,[^12][^13][^14] but a study of the children of twins found that anxiety passed from parents to adolescents through the environment, with no evidence of significant genetic transmission.[^15] Parenting accounts for only about 4% of the variance in child anxiety.[^16] In experiments, parents' spoken threat information strongly raised children's fear of new things, from 30 months to 18 years.[^17]

Over weeks and months, a large network study linked depression among friends and neighbours up to three degrees of separation,[^18] and a related study found the same for loneliness.[^19] Statisticians argue that this kind of data cannot tell contagion apart from people choosing similar friends,[^20] and that the analyses don't support the claims.[^21] A study using random roommate assignment found "modest evidence for anxiety and depression contagion" and no significant overall contagion of mental health.[^22] Adolescents' social anxiety rose over time when their friends were socially anxious.[^23] Between spouses after a job loss, anxiety crossed in both directions.[^24]

In groups, anxiety-type outbreaks of mass psychogenic illness typically start with sudden exposure to a perceived threat such as an odour, spread by sight, sound, and word of mouth, and often end once tests discredit the threat.[^25][^26] Disaster researchers have long reported that mass panic flight is rare,[^27] and crowd-psychology studies find that people in emergencies often cooperate.[^28]

Without face-to-face contact, adults with six or more hours a day of media coverage after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombings reported more acute stress than people who had been at or near the marathon.[^29] Frequent social media exposure early in the COVID-19 outbreak in China was linked to higher odds of anxiety.[^30] A negative news bulletin made viewers more anxious than positive or neutral ones in an experiment.[^31] A German clinic reported functional tic-like behaviour spreading through social media.[^32]

On who catches it, closeness raised the odds in the cortisol study,[^2] secure attachment strengthened the cortisol link between partners,[^33] and results for shared identity and for empathy conflict.[^34][^35][^36][^37] In rodents, an observer that watches another receive shocks freezes. Studies tie this to a circuit between the anterior cingulate cortex and the basolateral amygdala,[^38] and alarm odors and oxytocin also play a part.[^39][^40][^41] Whether this is empathy or simpler contagion is contested.[^42][^43]

## Groups and crowds

**How mass psychogenic illness starts, spreads, and ends.** A 2002 review by Bartholomew and Wessely in the British Journal of Psychiatry describes the pattern. Before 1900, reports were dominated by motor symptoms. In the 20th century, anxiety symptoms were set off by sudden exposure to an anxiety-generating agent, most often an odour or food-poisoning rumours. Wessely's two types are mass anxiety hysteria, which is short (typically one day) and follows sudden extreme anxiety over a perceived false threat, and mass motor hysteria, which builds slowly from pent-up stress in an intolerable setting such as a convent, school, or factory. The perceived danger needs only to be plausible.[^25] Spread happens through sight, sound, or word of mouth. It tends to move down the age scale, starting with older or higher-status students. It occurs in segregated or cohesive groups with a high proportion of females, and media publicity and rumour amplified it. In a Singapore school outbreak, those who accepted the idea succumbed and those indifferent to it were immune. At a California army barracks, people who witnessed "resuscitations" or others' symptoms were about three times more likely to report symptoms.[^25] Anxiety-type episodes resolved quickly, often once environmental or medical tests came back negative and discredited the threat. Closing the site was suggested to disperse the group. A Basel school episode subsided after school hours and relapsed when students re-entered school grounds. Convent motor outbreaks lasted months and sometimes waxed and waned for years.[^25] Cases the review lists include Loudun, France (1632 to 1634); Unterzell, Germany (1749); Mattoon, Illinois (1944, the "mad gasser"); the West Bank (1983, 947 residents reporting symptoms during poison-gas rumours); Singapore (1985, 65 students and a teacher after an unusual smell); Georgia (1989, about 400 adolescent girls); and a Maryland subway (2001, 35 treated after window cleaner was sprayed).[^25]

**Warren County High School, Tennessee, 1998.** On November 12, 1998, a teacher at the school in McMinnville noticed a "gasoline-like" smell, then had headache, nausea, shortness of breath, and dizziness. Symptoms soon spread to students in her room, the school was evacuated, and 100 people went to the emergency room that day; 38 were admitted overnight. The school reopened November 16, and on November 17, 71 more people with symptoms went to the emergency room.[^26][^60] The school had 1,825 students and 140 staff. More than 220 air samples, plus water, wipe, and soil samples and blood and urine tests, showed no toxin.[^26] Among 186 people in the first group, symptoms began in at least 49 locations, including 36 classrooms. 69% were female, against 52% of the school. 120 (65%) reported an odor, described with more than 30 different words.[^26] A questionnaire a month later (284 students in 16 classrooms) linked illness to female sex (relative risk 2.44), seeing another ill person (2.43), knowing a classmate was ill (3.44), and reporting an odor (3.51). Illness was not linked to age, grade, or location. Most symptoms resolved quickly with oxygen or removal from the school.[^26] The authors say intensive media attention probably heightened collective anxiety and may have contributed to the second cluster, and local media were still reporting on people with persistent headaches over a month later. They note that contagion is increased by proximity and "line of sight" transmission and that outbreaks more often affect girls and women. They also note that vigorous investigation may reinforce suspicion of a cover-up, and that announcing the diagnosis promptly is hard in practice because of the shame and anger it can cause. In this case, officials assured the public that tests were normal without mentioning a psychogenic cause, and the outbreak subsided.[^26]

**Le Roy, New York, 2011 to 2012.** In fall 2011, 12 students at Le Roy Junior-Senior High School developed involuntary motor and vocal tics resembling Tourette syndrome. After the first cases in October 2011, the school district worked with county and state health agencies and medical contractors. On January 11, 2012, the associate commissioner of health told a crowd of over 300 that a three-and-a-half-month investigation had "conclusively ruled out" infectious or environmental causes. The January 31 report said a pediatric neurologist had evaluated 8 of the 12 cases and concluded the cluster was conversion disorder. Drinking-water samples were tested for 58 volatile organic compounds, 63 pesticides and herbicides, and 11 metals, and investigators also considered the Gardasil vaccine and PANDAS.[^61] Many parents strongly opposed the diagnosis. Some wondered whether "toxic layering" from industrial sources, including a nearby Superfund site, was to blame, and Erin Brockovich's team arrived to take samples. Over three weeks from mid-January 2012, several girls appeared on TV and others posted videos to Facebook and YouTube. Two girls in Corinth, about 250 miles away, developed similar symptoms in the same period, and three more Le Roy students and a 36-year-old woman then came down with symptoms; one student in the second cluster was male. The neurologists called it mass psychogenic illness because of its purported spread among people who knew each other.[^61] The paper's authors, anthropologists Goldstein and Hall, say they do not diagnose the illness. They argue that the neurologists' brain-science expertise prevailed over environmental inquiry and that the diagnosis served professional and media interests. That is their critique.[^61]

**Spread through social media.** Fremer et al. (2022) studied 32 patients (mean age 20.1; 16 female) seen at a German Tourette clinic between May 2019 and September 2021 for functional Tourette-like behaviour after watching social media content. Their symptoms largely overlapped with those of the German YouTuber Jan Zimmermann, whom the authors call a "virtual" index case. The authors write that outbreaks of mass sociogenic illness were believed to require face-to-face contact, and they propose a new type spread through social media, "mass social media-induced illness." Symptoms typically began abruptly, at a mean age of 19. All patients had psychological stressors around the time of onset, unconscious inner conflicts, and/or structural deficits. 94% had other psychiatric symptoms (social behavior abnormalities 81%, obsessive-compulsive behavior 47%, anxiety 41%, depression 31%), and about 47% had been bullied. The authors conclude that existing psychiatric symptoms plus stressors predispose people to contagion. The paper calls the "mass social media-induced illness" label the authors' own hypothesis.[^32]

**Reactions to vaccination.** In December 2015, the WHO's Global Advisory Committee on Vaccine Safety reported clusters of anxiety-related reactions after vaccination in countries including Iran, Italy, India, Viet Nam, Australia, Taiwan (China), and the United States, in rural and urban settings, rich and poor countries, and with different vaccines. Reactions usually began within 15 minutes. Most clusters followed a new vaccine or a change in the routine programme, and a small cluster can spread quickly into a larger outbreak. Most reactions involved school-age children, and some studies found more girls affected than boys. In one cluster during a mass measles campaign in a European country, rumours and fears of "unknown events" spread through media and social networks and may have worsened the situation. WHO advises prompt recognition and assessment on site, and lists lack of recognition, delayed response, excessive hospitalization, and overreaction as things that can make it worse. In 2018 the committee endorsed the term "immunization stress-related responses."[^62]

**Crowd panic.** Quarantelli's 2001 paper "The Sociology of Panic" says most definitions treat panic as intense unfounded fear or as flight, and that current debates ask whether panic is irrational and whether it spreads readily from person to person. Two of the three major empirical studies he presents found panic flight to be rare and to lack many traits usually attributed to it, even where it would seem likely. He asks why the idea of panic persists in popular culture and among scholars, and whether the term still has scientific value.[^27] His 1975 paper, drawing on about 25 years of study, covers panic's features (its flight nature and its non-social and non-rational aspects) and gives rough indications of how often it occurs and what makes it more likely.[^63] His 2008 paper says more than fifty years of research establishes how people behave in disasters, and lists panic flight first among six areas where popular belief and research disagree.[^64]

**Crowds as a resource.** In a 2009 study, John Drury and colleagues interviewed 21 survivors of 11 emergencies, including the Hillsborough crush, the 1983 Harrods bomb, and a 2002 beach party overcrowding. Twelve described feelings of unity; nine said it was every person for themselves. Those who felt united reported a shared fate, more helping, including of strangers, and more orderly behaviour such as queuing to escape. The researchers argue shared identity makes the crowd "a crucial adaptive resource for survival."[^28] A 2013 survey found police endorsed parts of the mass-panic myth, including that fear and rumours spread through crowds like a contagion. Civilian planners and stewards held similar views, and students and the public held them even more strongly.[^65] A 2015 study of a near-disaster at an outdoor music event (survey of 48, interviews with 20) found that stronger identification with the crowd predicted feeling safer, and attendees and some professionals said the crowd's self-organization helped prevent disaster.[^66] A 2024 paper on the 1943 Bethnal Green tube shelter crush, which killed 173 people, analysed 85 witness statements. Perceptions matched a real threat, only a small minority misread the sound, and that misreading cannot explain why most of the crowd surged. The authors propose that flight follows shared norms, and that crowd density plus limited knowledge of obstructions and of expected patterns of entry can together produce a deadly crush.[^67]

## Disagreement / Uncertainty

**Contested claim: whether shared identity strengthens caught stress.** Schury, Häusser and Nater (2020) found observers released more cortisol when they shared an identity with the stressed person.[^34] A 2019 poster from overlapping authors found contagious stress that shared identity did not change.[^35]

**Contested claim: whether empathy makes people catch stress or anxiety.** Shu et al. (2017) found trait emotional empathy and empathic concern predicted vicarious anxiety while watching threat clips.[^37] Dimitroff et al. (2017) found empathy did not change the size of observers' heart responses, only how soon they peaked.[^36] Erkens et al. (2018) found no support for trait empathy as a moderator of contagious stress.[^77] In Gallistl et al. (2025), empathy did not change the attachment-cortisol link.[^33]

**Contested claim: the smell route in humans.** Mujica-Parodi et al. (2009) found stress sweat activated receivers' amygdala and improved their judgment of emotion in ambiguous faces.[^6] Gomes et al. (2023) could not replicate earlier findings that fear odors help people tell negative faces from neutral ones, two more attempts also failed, and the authors say results from this paradigm "should be interpreted cautiously."[^7]

**Failed replication: facial feedback.** The 2016 Registered Replication Report of the pen-in-teeth study found a 0.03-unit effect against 0.82 in the original.[^46] It tested facial feedback, not catching anxiety from another person.[^46]

**Uncertain: what bodily syncing means.** Its existence is established for skin conductance and heart activity, but its psychological nature is "still mostly unknown," and the studies have statistical and design problems.[^9] Palumbo et al. call the literature "fragmented."[^8] Terminology is inconsistent across the stress-contagion field,[^4] and 12% of emotional contagion articles gave no definition.[^1]

**Contested claim: genes in parent-to-child transmission.** Twin studies find a heritable share in anxiety.[^48][^12][^13][^14] The children-of-twins study found environmental transmission from parent to adolescent and no evidence of significant genetic transmission; its authors suggest genetic effects may differ between adolescence and adulthood.[^15] Psychiatric News says the article hedges that the link "appears to be" environmental, and that parental anxiety was self-reported.[^50]

**Uncertain: shared family environment by age.** Gregory and Eley say shared environment matters in children and adolescents but is generally not significant in adult studies.[^48] Garcia et al. found shared environmental effects negligible by late adolescence or early adulthood.[^14] Hettema et al. found shared family environment may have contributed to GAD in women.[^12]

**Uncertain: direction of the parenting link.** Van der Bruggen et al. report an association between parental control and child anxiety that does not settle which way it runs.[^51]

**Contested claim: network contagion.** Christakis, Fowler and colleagues report emotions linked across three degrees of separation.[^18][^19] Shalizi and Thomas argue contagion cannot be identified from such data without strong assumptions.[^20] Lyons argues the conclusions don't follow from the analyses.[^21] Gelman says the effects are not convincingly shown but this doesn't prove the authors wrong.[^59] The random-roommate study found mental health contagion "lower, or at least more context specific, than implied by the recent studies," with modest evidence for anxiety.[^22]

**Contested claim: the Le Roy diagnosis.** The associate commissioner of health announced that the investigation had "conclusively ruled out" infectious or environmental causes. A pediatric neurologist who evaluated 8 of the 12 cases concluded the cluster was conversion disorder, and the neurologists called it mass psychogenic illness. Many parents opposed the diagnosis, and the anthropologists who studied the case argue the diagnosis served professional and media interests.[^61]

**Authors' hypothesis: "mass social media-induced illness."** The German clinic's label for social-media spread is the authors' own hypothesis, from a single clinic's patient series.[^32]

**Expert consensus against popular belief: crowd panic.** Disaster research lists panic flight first among areas where popular belief and research disagree.[^64] Police, planners, stewards, students, and the public endorsed parts of the mass-panic myth.[^65]

**Contested claim: how large online contagion is.** Kramer et al. present their Facebook experiment as evidence of emotional contagion without in-person contact.[^71] Pew describes the effect as very small, and Kramer called it the "minimal amount to statistically detect it."[^72]

**Uncertain: direction of the worry-news link.** In Bounoua et al., only two of four cross-lagged paths were significant: worry predicted news use one month, and news use predicted worry the next.[^70] Gao et al. say their cross-sectional design makes cause hard to establish.[^30]

**Results that differ: parent-child stress in the moment.** Infants aged 12 to 14 months showed heart-rate increases matching their mothers' stress.[^80] Children aged 8 to 12 showed no evidence of mother-child stress covariation.[^81]

**Contested claim: whether rodent fear contagion is empathy.** Keysers et al. say rodents use brain structures that correspond to those in human empathy.[^42] Du et al. say it is unclear whether vicarious fear reflects emotional empathy, since vicarious freezing "might be emotional contagion without an intrinsic understanding of the distress source," and some rats that stop shocking a partner may be avoiding their own negative state.[^43] Carrillo et al. say it is open whether pain, fear, arousal, or salience drives the ACC responses.[^87] The link to human empathy is inferred from human brain imaging, because single-neuron recording is not possible in humans.[^87]

**Numbers that differ:**
- **Eley children-of-twins sample:** 385 identical and 486 fraternal families;[^15] 387 and 489;[^50] "about 1,000 families".[^49]
- **Gao COVID survey sample:** 4,872 and 4,827 in different places in the same paper.[^30]
- **Jarvis meta-analysis study count:** 43 studies in total, while the 33 and 7 it describes sum to 40.[^82]

## Useful Details

- Garcia et al. suggest targeting shared environmental factors before middle adolescence.[^14]
- The King's College London release advises on resilience and small age-appropriate risks; this is the authors' advice, not a tested outcome.[^49]
- Engert said TV programmes showing suffering could transmit stress to viewers.[^75]
- The Warren County outbreak cost 18,000 person-days lost to closure, more than $93,000 in medical charges, and over 3,000 person-hours of investigation across 12 government agencies.[^26]
- A survey in 12 lowand middle-income countries found fainting the most common event in vaccination stress reactions, and WHO notes that psychological labels for severe reactions could stigmatize the people vaccinated.[^62]
- The UC Irvine release recommends that the public limit repetitive coverage and speculation, and that news outlets avoid sensationalism and disturbing images.[^69]

## Open Research Questions

- Has the Engert et al. (2014) cortisol study been independently replicated?
- Is there a study measuring listeners' physiological response to an anxious voice?
- Is there evidence that posture carries anxiety from one person to another?
- Does the failed replication of the pen-in-teeth facial feedback study bear on emotional contagion?
- Has any network study of friends of friends measured anxiety, rather than depression or loneliness?
- Is there a longitudinal study of couples outside a crisis that tracks one partner's anxiety predicting change in the other's?
- Do coworkers catch anxiety from each other over weeks or months?
- Does the published version of the Quinn, Canevello and Crocker roommate study match its conference abstract?
- How often does mass panic flight occur? The abstracts read give no frequency figures.
- Has any study tested directly whether age changes how easily people catch anxiety?
- Are there studies of sex differences in catching anxiety beyond the ones here?
- What did Shu et al. (2017) find about trait anxiety?
- What does the Engert, Linz and Grant (2019) review conclude about who is more susceptible?
- Does the CRH pathway found in mice by the Bains lab also operate in people?
- Has the zebrafish oxytocin result been replicated or critiqued?
